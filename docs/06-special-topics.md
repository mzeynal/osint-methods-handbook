# Part 6. Special topics

## 6.1 Regional platforms and their features

Every region has its own information environment. An investigator who knows only "global" platforms misses most of the data. Below are general principles and a detailed example for China.

### General principles for an unfamiliar region

1. **Find out which platforms are used there.** They often differ from the ones you are used to.
2. **Bring in a native speaker of the language and context.** They will see code words, slang and subtext that machine translation will not understand.
3. **Allow for censorship and self-censorship.** What is missing from results may have been deleted or may be unsayable; the patterns of deletion are sometimes a clue in themselves.
4. **Understand naming norms.** The order of given and family names, patronymics, double surnames, transliterations.
5. **Know local registers and map services.** Global maps and registers are often incomplete.
6. **Remember the risks.** Data and security laws differ; in some countries work on certain topics is dangerous for people on the ground.

### Other regions (G: general pointers)

| Region | What matters |
| --- | --- |
| Russia and neighbouring countries | Local social networks and a search engine with strong coverage of regional sources; a major role for Telegram; Cyrillic transliteration in several systems (section 3.1); local registers and court databases |
| Middle East and North Africa | Arabic and its dialects; Telegram, X, Facebook; the way Arabic names are written; limits on access to data |
| Latin America | WhatsApp and Facebook as the main channels; double surnames; open registers differ by country |
| South and Southeast Asia | Facebook, TikTok, local messengers; multilingualism |
| Sub-Saharan Africa | WhatsApp, Facebook; limited indexing by search engines; the role of local media |

For any of these areas, start by identifying the platforms and languages, then find a native speaker of the context and local guides.

## 6.2 Conflict and human rights

Investigations of wars and human rights violations need especially strict procedures: the credibility of the material, whether it can be used in court, and people's safety depend on them.

### Documentation workflow

1. **Save at once.** Archive, download, compute the hash, record the source and time (section 2.2, section 8.1). Material is deleted quickly, especially from platforms that fight "violence".
2. **Establish the source and the first publication.** Who published first, where they got the file (section 4.1).
3. **Geolocation and chronolocation.** Where and when it was filmed (section 4.3).
4. **What is shown.** Write observations separately from conclusions: who, what, with what, where.
5. **Identification of weapons and equipment.** Determining the type of munition, projectile or vehicle from photographs and video.
6. **Identification of units.** Patches, equipment markings, numbers, speech.
7. **Independent confirmations.** Other angles, satellite imagery, testimony.
8. **A conclusion with a confidence scale and a description of limits.**

### Identifying weapons

Identifying explosive items and munitions from images is a separate discipline. Open-source groups publish guides to identifying ordnance in social media images; Bellingcat's guide and the Open Source Munitions Portal are starting points (S).

**An important warning.** Identifying a weapon from a photo is not an end in itself; an error can attribute a strike to the wrong actor. Always give the confidence level and the alternatives.

### Sound

Audio sometimes decides the conclusion: in one public geolocation challenge a recording that participants took for the sounds of combat turned out to be the call of a shoebill; matching against recordings of the region's fauna was used to check it (S). The lesson: first check what you are hearing, then draw conclusions.

### Preserving evidence and rules for courts

If the material may be used in an investigation or a court, follow recognised methods: the Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open Source Investigations (G) describes the requirements for collection, preservation, verification and chain of custody. Bellingcat has a Justice and Accountability unit that shows the applicability of open sources in legal proceedings (S). The practical minimum:

- hashes and a log;
- separate storage of originals;
- a record of who worked with the material and when;
- a description of methods sufficient for reproduction;
- care with image processing: record any changes (enlarging, correction) and never replace the original.

### Safety of people

- Do not publish information that could reveal the whereabouts of witnesses, journalists or civilians.
- Blur the faces and identifying features of minors and victims unless publication is necessary.
- Assess the risk of retaliation and secondary trauma.
- Warn about graphic material.

### Psychological safety

Material about violence leaves a mark even when you work "professionally". Practices that help:

1. Limit the time spent on heavy material and take breaks.
2. For verification use metadata and context; do not watch the whole thing unless needed; shrink the viewing window and turn off the sound.
3. Do not work on heavy cases alone; agree a way of talking things over with a colleague.
4. Watch for signs of overload: disturbed sleep, irritability, avoidance.
5. Agree in advance on the right to decline a case without explanation.
6. For persistent symptoms, see a specialist. Organisations working with investigators run seminars on this topic; for example Bellingcat held an open discussion on the risk of moral injury in open-source work (S).
